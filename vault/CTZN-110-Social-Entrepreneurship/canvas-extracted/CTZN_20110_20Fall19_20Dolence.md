Course Description:
This course uses an inquiry-based approach to introduce students to aspects of citizenship, with
an emphasis on the development of critical thought, civil discourse and applied ethical
reasoning. Sections of the course pose discipline-specific topics and questions to facilitate
students’ exploration of citizenship. Students will build and refine their skills for academic
success and will identify strengths and weaknesses in their oral communication.
Section Description:
What is a wicked problem? How can businesses contribute to ethical, just, and sustainable
solutions? How do/must we work across diverse disciplines and communities to find “outside of
the box” answers to the world’s most pressing challenges? This course explores the historical,
theoretical, and applied methods of social innovation, entrepreneurship, and their relationship to
ethical global citizenship. As students dig deep to discover their own purpose and responsibility
as global citizens, they will learn to harness their skills and power to implement visionary
solutions. Over the course of the semester, students will form strong teams that draw upon
multiple disciplines to research deeply rooted problems, connect to the strengths and passions
of individuals, and create game-changing solutions that will benefit their communities and the
world.
Course structure and approach:
This course is structured as a discussion seminar grounded in important questions around the
intersections of economy, entrepreneurship, and social purpose as they relate to the notion of
citizenship. The course will engage students in a continuous cycle of theory/practice and
reflection/action. Through classroom discussions, team-building exercises, creative visioning,
and reflection, students will acquire the tools to identify a problem at its root and form a
business idea/ plan and/or social innovation as a proposed solution.
Students in the course will be required to spend time out in the community and interact with
local business owners and community leaders. This course places a strong emphasis on public
speaking to a variety of audiences. Students will participate in the creation of a variety of textual
and artistic representations including market research maps, narratives, digital stories, business
pitch, and keeping a journal of the experience. Students are encouraged to have fun with this
creative learning environment, using their experiences and own life experiences as their canvas.

Citizen 110 Course Objectives:
Learning outcomes will be mapped to assignments as (CTZN)
At the end of this course students will be able to:
1. 	Identify and describe the relationships between a citizen’s individual rights and
responsibilities and the broader responsibility to the common good.
2. 	Demonstrate critical thinking by identifying, explaining, and analyzing arguments.

Public Narrative:
Students will be required to write two public narrative essays. These essays will consist
of thoughtful self-reflection about the ‘story of who you are in relation to broader public
communities and your sense of your responsibilities: your fundamental public values,
where they come from, defining moments in your life thus far, key events, insights,
people, traditions that orient you in the world, and so forth. We will talk extensively
about these essays at the beginning of the course. The first will be two pages in length
and is due early in the semester. The second paper will be four to five pages in length
and is due in the middle of the semester after students have had time to listen to and
discuss each other’s’ narratives in several ones to one setting. It is essentially a deepening
and extending of the first paper. The key questions will revolve around:
1. 	The story of “you”. Who are you at a deeper level
2. 	The story of “us”. What do you define as your community and your role in it?
3. 	The story of “now”. Why are the story of you and the story of us linked, and what
urgency do they carry?
