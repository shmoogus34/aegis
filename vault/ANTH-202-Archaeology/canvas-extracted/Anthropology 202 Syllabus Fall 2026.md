Course catalog description:
ANTH 202. Archaeology. This course introduces students to the theories and methods of archaeological
research. Topics covered in this course include, among others, oral communication in archaeology, scientific
enquiry in archaeological investigation, the history of archaeology, natural and cultural transformation
processes, geophysical methods of site identification, relative and chronometric dating techniques, settlement
analysis, burial analysis, environmental reconstruction, artifact analysis, bioarchaeology, cognitive archaeology,
archaeological theory and cultural resources management. Case studies will be used to illustrate how the
application of information gleaned through archaeological enquiry has relevance to significant contemporary
issues with regard to social, cultural, and/or ethical considerations.
Core Curriculum Foundation Course Requirements
In this Scientific Reasoning course, students will:
• Engage in creative inquiry and cultivate curiosity
• Develop foundational knowledge (disciplinary content) and skills in the discipline (e.g. how to
communicate, study, read, etc.).
Course objectives (Student Learning Outcomes - SLO):
By the end of this course, students will be able to:
1. Use scientific theories, scientific models, and empirical evidence to describe and make predictions about
natural phenomena.
2. Use appropriate scientific practices, including making observations, asking scientific questions,
formulating hypotheses, identifying relevant variables, planning and carrying out investigations,
evaluating data, and drawing evidence-based conclusions

DESCRIPTION OF MODULES AND ASSIGNMENTS
Speaking-infused assignment (Pillar SLO 4):
This class will be run as a discussion seminar and class discussion is expected every day. In order to do well in
this course, it is essential that students engage effectively in class discussions. To facilitate student learning in
this speaking infused component of the course, the professor will provide instruction on the importance of oral
communication skills in the field of archaeology during the first week of class. Students will also be provided
with concrete examples of how to achieve success in this element of the course. This will include guidance on
strategies to employ for effective synthesis of reading assignments into class discussions that will prove
informative to all and that will spur intellectual exploration of class topics. It is the aim of this process to
provide students with multiple points of feedback, both formally and informally, that will lead to continual
improvement in effective oral communication.
Three evaluations of student class discussion/engagement will be provided throughout the semester (see course
schedule). After each of these evaluations, there will be an in-class debriefing and discussion of the evaluations
and all students will be given guidance on ways to improve their class participation. A final evaluation of class
discussion will be the average of the three grades earned throughout the semester. Each of the first three
evaluations will provide students with feedback as to student performance within the parameters of the rubric
for class participation. These evaluations will provide students with multiple opportunities to improve
performance in class participation and oral communication in class. See the rubric for this at the end of this
syllabus.
Case-study approach:
An element of most class meetings will be a case study on a particular archaeological site or problem. These
case studies will be used to present students with information about how an archaeologist approached a
particular site or issue within the context of the material for that day. The professor will hold back important
pieces of information to challenge students to arrive at their own solutions to the problems by applying
scientific models and theories through class discussion. This will facilitate student development of skills related
to the scientific method and making predictions about natural phenomena. It is utterly critical that students
come to class prepared to engage in discussions.
This approach presents myriad opportunities for student development and growth intellectually within the
discipline as well as in oral communication skills. By probing the issues presented in the case study with a

room full of colleagues, each student will be challenged to push their intellectual curiosity and that of their
peers in ways that helps the whole class work through the problems presented and arrive at valid conclusions.
All the while, students will be required to demonstrate effective use of oral communication skills by adapting
their communication to the purpose, context and audience in ways that are specific to the discipline of
archaeology. These skills will be addressed in the first week of class and then reiterated throughout the
semester in informal and formal evaluations of class participation.
A template for taking notes on each case study is provided in this syllabus. This will be a useful tool for
students to consider each case study within the framework of the scientific method.

The Scientific Method – Archaeological Case Study Template
For each case study that we examine in class record the following information to see gain an understanding
of the application of the scientific method to archaeological problems
Case Study Name:
Observation - The investigator notes a phenomenon that poses a problem/question. What is/are
the key observation(s)?
Hypothesis Formulation - The investigator poses hypotheses that could potentially explain the observation.
What is/are the hypothesis(es) being examined?
What prediction(s) were made about the hypothesis(es)?
Experimental Design - The investigator designs an experiment which will yield data to either support or
refute one or more of the competing hypotheses.
What methods did the archaeologist use to test the hypothesis(es)?
Data Collection and Analysis – The fieldwork was completed and data are collected. What data resulted from
the field work? What did the analysis of the data demonstrate?
Conclusion - The investigator determines whether the outcome of the experiment refutes or supports the
hypothesis.
What did the archaeologist conclude about the hypothesis?
