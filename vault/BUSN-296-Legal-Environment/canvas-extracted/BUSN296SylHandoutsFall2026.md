Introduction to Law
Four major sources of law are:
1. U.S. Constitution (1788) – The Constitution is the supreme law of the land and established the structure of the government and the relationship of the government to the people.  It created three branches of government…legislative (Art I), executive (Art II) and judicial (Art 3).  It also limits the government’s ability to restrict individual rights.  Certain powers are specifically given to the federal government, and these are known as “enumerated powers”.  One very important provision states that any power not specifically granted to the federal government is a “reserved power” specifically granted to the states.

Valid Agreements
I.  Elements of a Contract
 Mutual Assent (Agreement) = Offer + Acceptance
1.  Offer – Expresses a willingness to enter into a contract.  The offeror is the person who makes the offer, and the offeree is the person to whom the offer is made.  Communication of the offer from the offeror to the offeree is a must. Offers made in jest are not enforceable. 
John D.R LEONARD v. PEPSICO, INC., 88 F. Supp. 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1999) http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U_n5SNrMaL8

 Revocation – An offer may be revoked at any time prior to it actually being accepted.  The exception to this rule is an “option contract”.  Offers may be revoked in the following situations: 
Lapse of time
Incapacity of offeror or offeree
Supervening Illegality
Destruction of the subject matter
Rejection revokes an offer.
2.  Acceptance – must be unequivocal. Note on “mailbox rule” – The mailbox rule only applies to acceptances; if applicable, an acceptance is effective the moment it is properly mailed.  http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posting_rule
3. Consideration – may be defined as the price paid for a promise.  Past consideration is insufficient. Note: There must be a new legal detriment…something you are not already obligated to do.
Capacity to Contract
I.  Minors – Lack capacity to contract.  As such, their contracts are voidable at their election.  
Generally, a minor must pay for the reasonable value of necessaries (food, clothing shelter).  
Quaere: What if the minor misrepresents his/her age?  Is the contract voidable by the other contracting party? 
Note:  By statute in a majority of jurisdictions, minors may not avoid education loans, medical bills, and contracts made while running a business. 
II. Mental Incapacity – The contracting parties must be mentally competent to enter into a valid contract.
What happens if one of the parties has been legally adjudicated incompetent?
What if one of the parties is mentally ill -- is it possible for a person to have a “lucid interval”?
III. Intoxication and Capacity – Intoxication rarely affects capacity to contract. When does intoxication affect capacity to contract?
IV. Defenses and Reality of Consent -- Is the offer and acceptance real or has there been duress, fraud, undue influence or a mistake?
Unilateral mistake generally does not affect the validity of a contract unless it is so big that the other contracting party knew or should have known that a mistake was being made.  Ex.  
Mutual Mistakes
Law – Mutual mistakes of law generally have no effect on the validity of a contract. Ex.  Zoning
Fact – a mutual mistake of fact voids a contract (i.e., Treasure Salvors case). FLORIDA DEPARTMENT OF STATE v. TREASURE SALVORS, INC., ET AL. Supreme Court of the United States, 458 U.S. 670 (1982)                                    http://www.admiraltylawguide.com/supct/TreasureSalvors.htm
C. Duress is a wrongful threat and makes an agreement voidable.  Note:  The threat of criminal prosecution is always a wrongful threat.  The threat of a civil suit is only duress if it is not based on a good faith claim.  Extreme duress creates a void contract.  Example: You are told to sign a contract at gunpoint.
D. Fraud in the inducement is the intentional misrepresentation of a material fact that is relied upon to the detriment of the relying party.  Fraud in the inducement creates a situation where the agreement is voidable, and the aggrieved party may sue for money damages for deceit.  There must be scienter (intent to deceive) and the misrepresentation must be a statement of fact. Note:  With an innocent misrepresentation, there is no scienter (intent).  This means that you can’t sue for damages; however, the agreement could be voided.
E. Fraud in the execution creates a void agreement.  This type of misrepresentation occurs in the form of the agreement.  Example: I tell you that you are signing your Sunday school pledge and really you are signing a promissory note.
F. Undue influence -- only occurs in a fiduciary relationship where great trust and confidence has been placed in someone (Example -- attorney/client).  With undue influence, the trusted party unduly influences the client.

Form of a Contract
A. The “Statute of Frauds” requires that certain types of contracts be evidenced by a writing   
Most S/F provide: “No action shall be brought in the following types of contracts unless the agreement or some memorandum or a note thereof shall be in writing and signed by the party to be charged.”  The contract is not void but is voidable.  
1.  The writing must:
a. Identify the contracting parties
b. Specify essential terms
c. Be signed by the party to be charged
Note: The memorandum need not be the actual contract.  It may be in several documents provided:
They are physically attached or
Each document is signed by the defendant or 
The unsigned ones are referred to in the signed ones or
It can be determined by their contents that they refer to the same transaction
2.  Effect of non-compliance - The oral promise is not enforceable
3.  Contracts that must be evidenced by “writing”:
Contracts not to be performed within a year.
Agreement to sell real property – Exception: If the purchaser goes into possession and makes substantial improvements.  
Promise to pay the debt of another. Note: Most states apply the ‘Main Purpose Doctrine’.  That is, if the promisor’s main purpose was to benefit himself, and the payment of the debt is incidental thereto, the promise is original and not within the S/F.
Contract of an executor to pay the debts of the decedent – The promise of the executor must be in writing to hold the executor personally liable on that promise.  
Promise in consideration of marriage (pre-nuptial agreements)
Contract for the sale of goods costing &gt; $500 (Art. 2 UCC) Exception: ‘Specially manufactured goods’ not suitable for resale.  
B. Parol Evidence Rule: The general rule is that spoken words (
parol
 evidence) will not be allowed to contradict or modify the terms of the written contract.  Additionally, a written contract that appears to be complete, may not be altered by written documents made prior to or at the same time the contract was executed unless they were incorporated by reference in the contract.  The reason for the rule:  Stability in commercial transactions.  http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parol_evidence_rule                                          
C.  Effect of illegality – An agreement that is illegal is void.  When an agreement is illegal, the parties are not entitled to the aid of the courts.  That is, the court will leave the parties in the positions it finds them.  
Crimes and Civil Wrongs – A contract to commit a crime or a civil wrong (such as infringe a patent) is treated like an illegal contract.
Good Faith and Fairness – The modern trend is for the courts to consider the elements of good faith and fairness. 
Unconscionability – If there is grossly disproportionate bargaining power, so that the weaker party cannot afford to risk confrontation, the courts may come to the aid of the weaker party.  
Marshall v. Mercury Finance Co., 550 So 2d 1026 (1989) In this case Marshall, a college graduate with a degree in Business Management, borrowed $5,000 from Mercury Finance at an interest rate of 29.48 percent.  Marshall challenged the validity of the contract on the ground that is unconscionable.  The court held that Marshall knew what he was getting into and therefore is bound by the contract.   
Legal Terminology -- Usury – occurs when money is loaned at a greater rate of interest than allowed by law.  http://www.lectlaw.com/files/ban02.htm

Commercial Paper/Negotiable Instruments
A. Introduction -- If you have a negotiable instrument that has been properly negotiated, then you apply the law of negotiable instruments.  Under the law of negotiable instruments, a holder of an instrument may have more rights under the instrument than the transferor.  
Example (w/o a negotiable instrument): Al represents to Bob that the car he has for sale has only been driven 50,000 miles.  Bob relied on Al’s representation and buys Al’s car for $500 and promises to pay Al $500 in two weeks.  Al lied…the car really has 150,000 miles on it.  Al transfers his rights under the contract with Bob to Charlie (an assignee).  Bob finds out about Al’s lie and refuses to pay the $500.  Does Bob owe Charlie?  NO.  There was fraud in the inducement.  Note: In the event that you do not have a negotiable instrument, then you apply the law of assignments, and the assignee has no more rights than the assignor would have under the instrument.  
Example (w/ a negotiable instrument): Using the same example as above…What if Bob gives Al a check for $500.  Al negotiates the check (endorses it to Charlie).  Now Bob refuses to pay the check that Charlie now holds.  Can Charlie collect from Bob?  YES…the check in this example is a negotiable instrument.  
Articles 3 and 4 of the UCC apply to two types of negotiable instruments:
1.  Draft--a written order from one party directing a second party to pay a third party.  A check is special type of a draft with the bank as the drawee.  It is always drawn on a bank and is payable on demand.
2.  Promissory note--a promise to pay a certain amount in money.  There are 2 parties.
A certificate of deposit (CD) is a two-party note.  It is a written acknowledgment of a receipt of money along with a promise to pay.

A.  Elements of a Bailment (Possession, Consent, Knowledge) -- There is no bailment unless the bailee obtains possession of the property with the intent to exercise control.
1. Possession:  Ex.  Plaintiff’s coat disappeared from a hook on a wall from the defendant’s restaurant.  Plaintiff didn’t notice the placard stating: “Not responsible for hats, coats...”  Neither the defendant nor any servant saw the coat.  Held:  No bailment -- the plaintiff didn’t intend to transfer possession.
2. Consent:  Ex.  B bought goods from S and claims they are defective.  B brings goods back to the store and leaves them without the consent of S.  S is not a bailee and is under no duty to exercise due care.
3. Knowledge:  Bailee must have knowledge of the presence of the goods.  Ex.  Concealed property in bailed property.  
Ex.  A coat with a fur piece concealed in it is deposited in a cloakroom that has an attendant. The fur piece is not returned.  Is the owner of the cloakroom liable?

Copyright Law – A copyright is the exclusive right of the creator of a literary or artistic work to use or reproduce it.  After the copyright expires, the work is in the public domain.  There have been major changes to our copyright law:
1989 – U.S. signed the Berne Convention. This is the copyright law followed in most developed countries of the world.  The most significant change it made to our law is that notice of copyright is no longer required; however, it is advisable to note that a work is copyrighted to defeat a claim of innocent infringement.  A copyright is the only type of intellectual property right in which registration is permissive…not mandatory.
Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998 (Mickey Mouse copyright law) added 20 years to existing copyrighted works.  Thus, an individual’s copyright is valid for the life of the creator plus 70 years.  A copyright held by a corporation lasts for 95 years from the date of publication or 120 years from the date of creation.
Copyright infringement occurs with unauthorized use of copyrighted material.  The fair use doctrine permits certain limited unauthorized use of copyrighted materials:
Quotation for review or criticism.
Use in parody or satire.
Brief quotation in news report.
Reproduction by a teacher or a student in connection with a lesson.
Reproduction for legislative or judicial proceeding.
