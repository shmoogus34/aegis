Commercial Paper/Negotiable Instruments (UCC Art. 3 &amp; 4)
Step 1 -- Is this a negotiable instrument?  The answer to this question is important because the holder of a negotiable instrument may have more rights under the instrument than the transferor.  
Two Examples
Al enters into a written contract to sell a car to Bob.  Bob promises to pay Al $5000 in 2 weeks.  Al lied to Bob -- the car’s mileage is 150,000 NOT 15,000.  Al assigns his contract rights to Charlie.  Can Charlie enforce the contract with Bob? 
No.  The assignee (Charlie) “stands in the shoes” of Al.
Same example only Bob gives Al a check.  Al transfers the check to Charlie through proper negotiation.  Can Charlie collect on the $5000 check?
Yes!  The check is a negotiable instrument.style.visibility
Drafts and Checks
Draft -- A three party instrument that is payable at a specified time.  A check is a type of a draft which is always drawn on a bank and payable on demand.

Note
Note -- A two party instrument containing a promise to pay a sum certain in money. 
   June 1, 2025
	I promise to pay to the order of  M. Flanigan $1000 plus 5% interest on June 1, 2030.
						Claire La Roche
A Certificate of Deposit is considered to be a type of a note.

Face of the Instrument
Words of negotiability (I promise to pay OR Pay to the order of OR to the bearer of ) must be on the face of the instrument UNLESS it’s a check.
A check can say “Pay to” and still be negotiable.
Order and Bearer Paper
Order paper is payable to a specific person. 
Bearer  paper is payable to Cash or Bearer or may be created when an instrument is endorsed in blank (only the endorser’s signature.
Step 2 -- Proper Negotiation
Order Paper -- delivery plus endorsement
Bearer Paper -- mere delivery is enough and no additional signatures are necessary.  Bearer paper is essentially a substitute for cash.  Example:  Bertha gets her paycheck every Friday.  She turns it over and immediately endorses it.  Should Bertha do this?
No…she has created bearer paper and a thief could steal it and cash it without any further endorsements.  
Hint: First look at the back of the instrument.  If there are no endorsements, then look at the face.  Endorsements can change the nature of the paper.
Endorsements
Restrictive endorsement -- places restrictions on the transfer of the paper.  For ex. “For deposit only”
Special endorsement – payable to a second person (“order paper”).
Qualified endorsement -- “Without Recourse” -- destroys any sort of contractual liability. Endorser has no responsibility if the check bounces.  
In blank – Only endorser’s signature.  “Bearer paper” and may be cashed by anyone holding the check.  
Holder in Due Course
After you determine that you have a negotiable instrument that has been properly negotiated (transferred), you need to determine whether the holder is a holder in due course.
A holder in due course is a good faith purchaser of a negotiable instrument who takes it without notice of a defense.  Was the check dishonored? Is it overdue (stale)? Is the underlying obligation based on a breach of contract or a fraudulent transaction?  
If you have a holder in due course, the hdc takes the negotiable instrument free of most defenses.
Example: Holder in Due Course
HDC takes a negotiable instrument free of most defenses.  The HDC must give value for the negotiable instrument and take it without notice of a defense.  
Example: Al gives Bob a $1000 note to pay for a flat screen TV.  Bob then sells the note to Charlie for $850.  Bob never delivers the TV to Al.  
What has just happened?
Can Charlie get payment from Al?  Yes.
What if Bob gives the note to Charlie…is Charlie a hdc?  No…he didn’t give value.
What if  Bob tells Charlie about his plan to “rip off” Al before he sells the note to Charlie.   Now can Charlie be a hdc?  No…he didn’t take the note without notice of a defense.  
Bank and Customer
Post dated checks – In general, the bank has no liability for paying a postdated check before the date on the instrument. To hold the bank liable:
Bank must be told.
Customer must have a genuine loss.
Stale Checks – A check is presumed to be “stale” after 6 months.
A bank may honor a stale check but is not required to do so. 
Stop Payment
Stop Payment Orders – You must give a bank a sufficient amount of time to implement a stop payment order.
Oral 14 days/Written 6 months
Certified Check – You may not stop payment on a certified check.
