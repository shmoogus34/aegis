Miranda Warnings are Not Given
If Miranda warnings are not given before custodial interrogation, any statements made by the suspect during that questioning cannot be used as evidence against them in a criminal trial.
Response from Congress -- 18 USC Section 3501 – a statement given without Miranda warnings may not be excluded provided it was voluntary.
Dickerson v. US – Held:  Section 3501 is unconstitutional.  “we do not think there is justification for overruling Miranda.  We conclude that Miranda announced a constitutional rule that Congress may not supersede legislatively.”
Exigent Circumstances
A warrant is not required due to an immediate threat to public safety, or a risk that evidence will be destroyed if the search is delayed. The following are examples:
Hot Pursuit -- If police are chasing a suspect and they enter a business, they can enter without a warrant. 
Emergency/Danger to persons -- If there's a reasonable belief that someone is in danger, they can enter the business to ensure safety. For instance, an emergency such as a fire. 
Other Exceptions to a Warrant
“Plain View” -- If a law enforcement officer is lawfully in a place where they can see evidence in plain view, they can seize it without a warrant. 
Consent -- The owner or authorized representative of a business can consent to a search, waiving the warrant requirement. 
Consequences of an Illegal Search
In Mapp v. Ohio, the US Supreme Court held that evidence seized pursuant to an illegal search is inadmissible in both state and federal court.  
Dolly Mapp was arrested for possessing obscene materials.
The Supreme Court said that the warrantless search was illegal. Furthermore, in this case, an exception to the requirement of a warrant did not apply.
The evidence was excluded at trial.

Probable Cause
A legal search may also be based on probable cause that exists at the time of the search.
Probable cause exists when there's a reasonable belief that a crime has been committed or is about to be committed, and that evidence or contraband will be found in a specific location. This can be established through police observation, witness statements, or other evidence.

Crimes of Force and Crimes Against Property
Larceny – the wrongful taking and carrying away the personal property of another with the intent to deprive the owner of the property.
Shoplifting is a statutory form of larceny.
‘Shoplifting’
The government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the person intentionally took possession of the item with the intent to permanently deprive the store of its value. 
Misdemeanor -- Petit Larceny items worth less than $1,000, carries a maximum penalty of 12 months in jail and a $2,500 fine. 
Felony -- Grand Larceny, items valued at $1,000 or more, can result in one to 20 years in prison and a $2,500 fine.

Robbery
Robbery is the unlawful taking of personal property from the possession or presence of another by means of force or putting the possessor in fear. 
Criminal Procedure and Businesses
Fourth A – protects individuals and businesses from unreasonable searches and seizures. When it is legal to search?
Search warrant -- A search warrant is a legal document issued by a judge that authorizes law enforcement to search a specific location for specific items. It requires probable cause, meaning a reasonable belief that a crime has been committed and that evidence of that crime will be found at the location.
